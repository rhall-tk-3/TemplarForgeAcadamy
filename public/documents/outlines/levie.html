✝ Part I — KTKC Identity, Rules & Formation

Weeks 1–4: Christian identity, rules and statutes, conduct, and the Five Governing Pillars

Week 1

Identity, Purpose, and Consecration

✝ Objectives

  • State the name and consecration of the Order
  • Explain the Christian nature and mission of the KTKC
  • Understand the Levie class period as an 8-week or 60-day probationary program
  • Identify general principles of promotion and timelines

✦ Lesson

Week 1 establishes the Christian identity of the Order, its consecration to Jesus Christ, and its mission of truthful Christian service. The Knights Templar of the Kingdom of Christ is consecrated to Jesus Christ and to the Holy Trinity. Its purpose is truthful, nonviolent Christian witness and service — not prestige, ceremony, or personal gain. The Order embraces Ecumenical Fellowship: brothers and sisters from across the Christian tradition are welcome, united by faith in Jesus Christ rather than divided by denominational difference. The Levie class period is an 8-week or 60-day probationary program. This is not a formality — it is an intentional formation period during which a new member demonstrates the seriousness of their commitment through attendance, study, and conduct. Promotion timelines exist to ensure that every member who advances has genuinely prepared. No one is rushed forward; no one is left behind without reason. The first week's work is to understand why you are here and what this Order is consecrated to.

⚔ Key Terms

  • KTKC — Knights Templar of the Kingdom of Christ — the modern Order in which the Levie program is conducted
  • Holy Trinity — The Christian doctrine of God as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; the foundation of the Order's consecration
  • Ecumenical Fellowship — The welcoming of Christians from across denominational traditions into shared brotherhood and sisterhood

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT State the consecration of the Order and the purpose it serves.
  • SHORT What is the Levie class period, and how long does it last?
  • ESSAY What does Ecumenical Fellowship mean, and why does the Order practice it?
Week 2

Standards, Duties, and Equality

✝ Objectives

  • Understand Ecumenical Fellowship and the rejection of false worship
  • Explain liberty of conscience and moral obedience
  • Identify the standards governing oaths, vows, and solemn promises
  • State the equality, attendance, and promotion requirements

✦ Lesson

Week 2 develops the Levie's understanding of what it means to be a Christian fellowship that rejects corrupt practice and guards the liberty of conscience of every member. The Order rejects false worship and all practices that contradict Scripture or lawful conduct. Members are not required to abandon their conscience — in fact, the Order protects liberty of conscience as a foundational principle. No member may be asked to bind themselves to a secret oath or to swear in ways that contradict Christian truth. Open promises and solemn vows, made honestly before God and witnesses, are the standard. Equality is not optional in this Order. Ranks cannot be bought or sold. No member receives advancement because of social status, wealth, family connection, or favoritism. Promotion requires completion of classwork and regular attendance — period. Round Tables — the assembly structure of the Order — exist as a place of equal standing: every member's voice has weight. Members are accountable to Christian truth, Scripture, and lawful civil conduct. This is the standard from the first day of the Levie class to the last day of the member's active service.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Equality and Fairness — The principle that no member may be treated differently based on wealth, status, or social standing
  • Round Tables — The assembly structure of the Order in which members meet with equal standing and shared voice
  • Probationary Period — The 8-week or 60-day Levie class period during which attendance, conduct, and classwork are evaluated for promotion

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT Why can ranks not be bought or sold in the Order?
  • SHORT What is liberty of conscience, and how does the Order protect it?
  • ESSAY Explain what it means to be accountable to Scripture and lawful conduct. Give a specific example.
Week 3

Conduct, Leadership, and Accountability

✝ Objectives

  • Understand the meaning of offices, titles, and rank as service
  • Explain the requirement of financial integrity and rejection of bought rank
  • Apply the principle of equality of persons and mutual respect
  • Describe daily duties, conduct expectations, and recordkeeping

✦ Lesson

Week 3 teaches the Levie to view rank as service rather than privilege. An office or title in the Order is not a reward for loyalty — it is a responsibility accepted on behalf of the whole community. Financial integrity is a defining standard: no member may buy, sell, or trade rank or position. The Order's resources belong to the mission, not to individuals. Offices exist to serve the members, not to enrich or elevate those who hold them. Equality of persons is not merely a rule — it is an expression of the Christian conviction that every human being is made in the image of God. No member, regardless of rank, is exempt from correction, accountability, or the respect owed to every other person in the Order. Daily duties are real: attendance is tracked, conduct is observed, and records are kept. These are not bureaucratic inconveniences. They are the concrete expressions of commitment. A member who cannot be counted on for ordinary duties will not be trusted with greater ones. The liberty of conscience principle means that no superior may command what violates Scripture or civil law. Leaders are held to a higher standard of visible virtue and humility — not a lower one. No rank shields a member from discipline. Understanding complaint and redress as a function of integrity — not rebellion — is essential for every Levie.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Liberty of Conscience — The right of every member to refuse commands that violate Scripture or civil law without fear of unjust punishment
  • Discipline and Redress — The Order's system for correction and complaint — tools of integrity, not rebellion
  • Standard of Leadership — The principle that those in higher rank are held to a higher — not lower — standard of conduct and humility

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT What does it mean that rank is service rather than privilege? Give a concrete example.
  • SHORT Under what circumstances may a member refuse a superior's command?
  • ESSAY Explain the difference between discipline as correction and discipline as punishment. Why does this distinction matter?
Week 4

The Five Governing Pillars

✝ Objectives

  • Name and define the Five Governing Pillars of the KTKC
  • Understand spiritual warfare as spiritual witness rather than physical combat
  • Explain brotherhood and sisterhood in charity as sacrificial fellowship
  • Assess personal readiness for promotion and probationary review

✦ Lesson

Week 4 gathers the moral and spiritual core of the entire Levie program into one central framework: the Five Governing Pillars of the KTKC. These pillars are not categories of bureaucratic policy — they are the living expression of what the Order is and what every member is called to become. The Five Governing Pillars are: (1) Service to Christ and His Kingdom — the Order exists for Christ, not for its members' advancement; (2) Prayer and Holy Meditation — the interior life of the Order is the source of its outward strength; (3) Discipline and Virtue — formation is a daily, intentional practice, not a one-time event; (4) Courageous Christian Witness — speaking and living the truth of the Gospel in the face of opposition; (5) Brotherhood and Sisterhood in Charity — sacrificial fellowship that bears one another's burdens. Spiritual warfare in this Order means spiritual witness: prayer, truth-speaking, living faithfully, and serving sacrificially. It is not about physical conflict. Promotion readiness is evaluated against these pillars. A Levie who can name them, define them, and give evidence of their practical application is ready to advance. The transition from Week 4 to Week 5 marks a shift from internal formation to historical knowledge — the second half of the program places that formation in historical context.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Five Governing Pillars — The five core commitments of KTKC: Service to Christ, Prayer and Holy Meditation, Discipline and Virtue, Courageous Christian Witness, and Brotherhood/Sisterhood in Charity
  • Spiritual Warfare — In the KTKC context: the practice of prayer, truth, faithful living, and sacrificial service as spiritual witness — not physical combat
  • Sacrificial Love — The willingness to bear one another's burdens and serve at personal cost; the heart of brotherhood and sisterhood in the Order

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT List and briefly define each of the Five Governing Pillars.
  • SHORT What does the Order mean by 'spiritual warfare,' and why is the distinction important?
  • ESSAY Which of the Five Governing Pillars do you find most challenging personally? Explain why and how you will strengthen it.

✝ Part II — Early Templar History

Weeks 5–8: Pre-Templar context, founding, recognition, and religious-order identity

Week 5

The Need for Protection (Early Pre-Templar History)

✝ Objectives

  • Explain why the Holy Land mattered to medieval Christians
  • Describe the pilgrimage routes and the dangers on the roads to Jerusalem
  • Summarize the First Crusade context from 1095 to 1099
  • Connect the danger faced by pilgrims to the need for organized protection

✦ Lesson

Week 5 begins the historical portion of the Levie program. The first four weeks established who we are as an Order. The final four weeks establish where we come from historically. These are not separate subjects — the history illuminates the identity. The Holy Land — and especially Jerusalem — held singular importance for medieval Christians. It was the city of Christ's crucifixion, burial, and resurrection. Pilgrimage to Jerusalem was not tourism: it was an act of profound religious devotion, often undertaken at great personal sacrifice. The roads from Europe to Jerusalem ran through territories that were unstable, dangerous, and poorly policed. Pilgrims were regularly robbed, attacked, enslaved, or killed. The First Crusade ran from 1095 to 1099. Pope Urban II's call at Clermont in 1095 set in motion a massive military expedition that succeeded in capturing Jerusalem in 1099. But success did not produce safety. The Crusader states established after 1099 were fragile — small, thinly populated Christian enclaves surrounded by far larger hostile forces. Travel remained dangerous even after the city fell to Crusader control. Pilgrims who arrived expecting the safety of Christian territory found roads that were still lethal. Pilgrim danger created the need for organized protection. This is the historical context that made the founding of a military-religious order not just understandable but necessary.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Christian Pilgrims — Medieval Christians who traveled to Jerusalem and the Holy Land as acts of religious devotion, often at great personal risk
  • First Crusade (1095–1099) — The military expedition called by Pope Urban II at Clermont in 1095 that culminated in the capture of Jerusalem in 1099
  • Jerusalem — The holy city central to Christian pilgrimage; site of Christ's crucifixion, burial, and resurrection; focal point of the Crusades

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT Why did medieval Christians consider Jerusalem worth traveling great distances to reach?
  • SHORT Why were Crusader states described as 'fragile,' and what did that mean for pilgrims?
  • ESSAY Explain how the history of the First Crusade creates the context for the founding of a protective order. Connect the historical need to the Order's present mission of service.
Week 6

Founding People and Places

✝ Objectives

  • Identify Hugh de Payns and the first companions of the Order
  • Explain the role of King Baldwin II and the Kingdom of Jerusalem
  • Describe the Temple Mount as the Order's early headquarters
  • State the founding purpose: the protection of pilgrims

✦ Lesson

Week 6 introduces the figures and places most closely connected to the beginning of the Order. Hugh de Payns is the founder most closely linked with the founding of the Knights Templar. Around 1119 or 1120, Hugh de Payns gathered a small group of knights — the first companions — and proposed to King Baldwin II of Jerusalem that they form a dedicated force to protect pilgrims on the roads. King Baldwin II received them and granted them quarters on the Temple Mount in Jerusalem — the site of the ancient Temple of Solomon — as their base of operations. This grant of headquarters was both practical and symbolic: it placed the new Order at the heart of the holy city and gave them a name. Their early mission was specific and humble: patrol the roads, escort pilgrims, and prevent the attacks that were destroying the lives of travelers. The founding purpose was not glory, power, or territorial conquest — it was protective service. The location, the founders, and the purpose together define the beginning of the Order. A Levie who cannot identify Hugh de Payns, King Baldwin II, and the Temple Mount does not yet have the factual foundation for the later weeks.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Hugh de Payns — The primary founder of the Knights Templar, who gathered the first companions and proposed the Order to King Baldwin II around 1119–1120
  • King Baldwin II — The King of Jerusalem who received Hugh de Payns and his companions and granted them quarters on the Temple Mount
  • Temple Mount — The site in Jerusalem of the ancient Temple of Solomon; the location of the early Order's headquarters, from which the Templars took their name

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT Who founded the Knights Templar, and approximately when?
  • SHORT What role did King Baldwin II play in the early history of the Order?
  • ESSAY Explain the significance of the Temple Mount as the early headquarters of the Order. Why does the location matter for understanding the name?
Week 7

Official Approval and Early Support

✝ Objectives

  • Explain the significance of the Council of Troyes
  • Describe the role of Bernard of Clairvaux as supporter — not founder
  • Distinguish formal approval from the earlier informal beginning
  • Explain how early mission and public trust were established

✦ Lesson

Week 7 shows how the early Order moved from informal beginning to formal recognition. The Order's founding around 1119–1120 was informal: a small group of knights with a mission but no official standing. For approximately a decade, they operated without formal church recognition or a written rule. In 1128 or 1129, the Council of Troyes changed that. The Council of Troyes was a church council convened in France that gave the Knights Templar formal approval and provided them with an official rule of life. This formal approval gave the Order legitimacy: the authority to recruit, to hold property, to receive donations, and to operate across Christian Europe under recognized ecclesiastical sanction. Bernard of Clairvaux was one of the most influential church figures of the era. He was a powerful supporter of the Knights Templar and is closely associated with their early rule and public reputation. However, Bernard of Clairvaux was a supporter — not the founder. The Council of Troyes granted formal approval; the actual founding had occurred a decade earlier under Hugh de Payns. This distinction matters: formal approval is not the same as informal beginning, and an important supporter is not the same as a founder. A Levie must be able to state clearly who founded the Order, what the Council of Troyes accomplished, and why Bernard of Clairvaux is significant without confusing these roles.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Council of Troyes (1128/1129) — The church council that gave the Knights Templar formal recognition and provided them with an official rule of life
  • Bernard of Clairvaux — A highly influential church leader who strongly supported the early Knights Templar and helped establish their public legitimacy; a supporter, not a founder

📜 Exam Questions

  • SHORT What did the Council of Troyes accomplish for the Knights Templar?
  • SHORT Why is it important to distinguish between Bernard of Clairvaux as a supporter and Hugh de Payns as the founder?
  • ESSAY Explain the difference between the informal founding of the Order around 1119–1120 and its formal recognition at the Council of Troyes. Why do both events matter?
Week 8

Religious-Order Identity and Early Timeline Review

✝ Objectives

  • Explain what distinguishes a religious order from an ordinary army
  • Describe the role of prayer, discipline, and service in Templar identity
  • Review the early timeline from 1095 through the Council of Troyes (1128/1129)
  • Prepare for the final written quiz and oral board review

✦ Lesson

Week 8 completes the Levie historical introduction by reviewing the early timeline and clarifying what separated the early Templars from an ordinary medieval army. An ordinary army exists to fight and be disbanded. A religious order exists to pray, serve, and persist. The Knights Templar were a religious body before they were a military one. Their daily life included the Hours of prayer, obedience to a rule, communal discipline, and a spiritual mission that extended beyond any single battle or campaign. This is what the Levie program has been building toward: an understanding that the Order the member is joining is not primarily a fraternal organization with military aesthetics — it is a Christian religious order with a living tradition of prayer, discipline, and service. The early timeline in review: 1095 — Pope Urban II calls the First Crusade at Clermont. 1099 — Jerusalem falls to Crusader forces. 1119 or 1120 — Hugh de Payns and the first companions begin what will become the Knights Templar. 1128 or 1129 — The Council of Troyes grants formal approval. The Levie program stops at this point. Later history — the growth, wealth, controversy, and suppression of the medieval Order — belongs to more advanced study. The Levie exits the program with a clear understanding of why the Order was founded, who founded it, how it gained recognition, and what kind of body it was. That is sufficient — and it is essential. Finish your reading log. Present it to the Schoolmaster. You are ready to advance.

⚔ Key Terms

  • Religious Order Identity — The defining character of the Knights Templar as a body of prayer, discipline, and service — not merely a military force
  • Service Before Self — The foundational ethic of the Order: members exist to serve the mission, not to be served by it

  • SHORT Name three things that distinguished the early Knights Templar as a religious order rather than an ordinary army.
  • SHORT Place these events in correct chronological order: Council of Troyes, First Crusade, Founding by Hugh de Payns, Capture of Jerusalem.
  • ESSAY Write a summary of what you have learned in the Levie program. Include both the internal (identity/rules) and historical portions, and explain how they connect to your personal commitment as a member.
